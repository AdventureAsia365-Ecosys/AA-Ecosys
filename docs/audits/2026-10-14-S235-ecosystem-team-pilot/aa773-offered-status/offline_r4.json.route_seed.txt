Why it matters: encounter traditional ways of life
Place background (checked facts about the place itself — you may use these; they say nothing about what this trip does):
- Lake Khövsgöl is a large freshwater lake in northern Mongolia, near the Russian border, and is one of the largest lakes in Central Asia by volume.
- It holds an estimated 70 percent of Mongolia's fresh water and about 0.4 percent of the world's fresh surface water.
- The lake sits at an elevation of roughly 1,645 metres (5,397 feet) above sea level in a mountain-rimmed basin.
- It lies at the ecological transition zone between Central Asian steppe grasslands to the south and Siberian taiga (boreal forest) to the north, giving the area unusually high biodiversity.
- The surrounding area is protected as Khövsgöl Nuur National Park, established in 1992.
- The Tsaatan (Dukha) people, a small community of reindeer herders, live in the remote taiga around the lake's northern shores and are among the last reindeer-herding peoples in the world.
- Visitors typically arrive via the nearby town of Khatgal at the lake's southern tip, which serves as the main gateway and base for boat trips and horse trekking.
- The lake freezes solidly in winter, typically from January through April, and was historically used as an ice road for trucks transporting goods.
- Summers are short and cool; sudden storms can arise quickly on the lake, making navigation in small boats potentially hazardous.

Why it matters: encounter one of Mongolia's most distinct ethnic groups
Place background (checked facts about the place itself — you may use these; they say nothing about what this trip does):
- The Tsaatan (Dukha) are a small semi-nomadic indigenous community, numbering only a few hundred people, who herd reindeer in the remote taiga forest of northern Khövsgöl Province.
- Their settlements are not permanent villages but seasonal camps of teepee-style dwellings called 'urts' (similar to Siberian chums), which are moved several times a year as the reindeer graze.
- The Tsaatan are ethnically and linguistically related to Tuvan peoples of southern Siberia, and their reindeer-herding culture is one of the last of its kind in the world.
- Their territory lies deep in the taiga, typically reached from the town of Tsagaannuur, and access requires a multi-hour horse or reindeer ride, often crossing rivers and boggy forest terrain.
- Reindeer in Tsaatan culture are used for riding and as pack animals as well as for milk; herds are not primarily raised for meat, making this practice distinct from many other reindeer-herding traditions.
- Visitors typically see the teepee camps, interact with families, observe reindeer up close, and may witness shamanistic practices, as shamanism remains an important part of Tsaatan spiritual life.
- The region experiences extremely cold winters and the taiga terrain is marshy in summer; visitors should be prepared for unpredictable weather and physically demanding travel regardless of season.
- Access to the eastern and western Taiga camps where Tsaatan live requires a permit issued through the local sum (district) government office in Tsagaannuur.
- The Tsaatan community has faced pressures including population decline, restrictions on cross-border movement with Russia, and the social and economic challenges of maintaining a traditional herding lifestyle.
